# How to Address Barriers to Home Care Access in Maryland

When a patient is ready to leave the hospital or skilled nursing facility, the goal is straightforward: get them home safely and keep them there. But **home care access in Maryland** is rarely as simple as writing a referral. For discharge planners and social workers across Montgomery County and the broader state, real barriers — financial, logistical, cultural, and informational — slow that transition every single day.

This article is for the professionals on the front lines of that work. Here is a practical look at what gets in the way, and what you can do about it.

---

## Why Home Care Access Matters More Than Ever

Maryland's population is aging. The CDC reports that adults 65 and older are the fastest-growing age group in the United States, and Maryland is no exception. Hospitals face mounting pressure to reduce readmissions, and research consistently shows that well-coordinated home care significantly lowers the risk of patients returning within 30 days.

When patients can't access home care promptly — or at all — the consequences ripple outward: longer hospital stays, strained family caregivers, unnecessary skilled nursing facility placements, and declining patient outcomes. Removing barriers to home care isn't just about convenience. It's a clinical and social imperative.

---

## The Most Common Barriers — and How to Address Them

### 1. Financial and Insurance Confusion

Cost is the barrier families raise most often, and it's frequently rooted in confusion rather than actual inability to pay.

Many families don't know that **Maryland Medicaid** programs — including the Community First Choice (CFC) option and waiver programs — may cover personal care and companion services for eligible individuals. Others assume Medicare covers ongoing home care when it typically covers only short-term skilled nursing or therapy following a qualifying hospital stay.

**What you can do:**
- Confirm insurance coverage early, ideally before discharge planning begins.
- Connect families to Maryland's Medicaid eligibility resources or a benefits counselor.
- Partner with home care agencies that have clear, written explanations of what each payer covers — and what it doesn't.
- Our blog post [A Guide to Medicaid and Medicare Coverage for Home Care in Maryland](/blog/a-guide-to-medicaid-and-medicare-coverage-for-home-care-in-maryland) is a resource you can share directly with families.

### 2. Delayed or Incomplete Referrals

Referrals that arrive without adequate clinical detail — or that arrive the day before discharge — make it extremely difficult for home care agencies to mobilize quickly. Missing information about diagnoses, functional limitations, medication regimens, or home environment creates gaps that delay service start.

**What you can do:**
- Use standardized referral packets that include functional status, primary and secondary diagnoses, fall risk, cognitive status, and emergency contacts.
- Build relationships with home care agencies *before* a discharge is imminent, so there's already a communication channel in place.
- For complex cases — stroke recovery, post-surgical care, dementia — flag them early and loop in the agency as soon as the discharge trajectory is clear.

### 3. Caregiver and Family Reluctance

Sometimes the patient is willing but the family is not — or vice versa. Family members may feel guilty about "letting someone else" care for their loved one, or they may underestimate how much professional support is needed. Patients themselves sometimes resist, interpreting home care as a loss of independence.

This reluctance is especially common in communities across Montgomery County, Rockville, and Gaithersburg where multigenerational households are the norm and the idea of outside caregivers can feel culturally uncomfortable.

**What you can do:**
- Frame home care as *extending* a family's ability to care, not replacing it. A caregiver coming in four hours a day often makes it possible for a spouse or adult child to remain the primary emotional anchor without burning out.
- Use plain, non-clinical language. "Someone will be there to help your mom get dressed safely and have company in the afternoon" lands differently than "personal care services."
- Our article on [the signs it's time to hire a caregiver](/blog/signs-its-time-to-hire-a-caregiver) can help families recognize needs they may be minimizing.

### 4. Geographic and Transportation Gaps

Not all areas of Maryland have equal access to home care. While agencies serving Silver Spring, Takoma Park, and central Montgomery County are plentiful, families in more rural or semi-rural pockets may face longer wait times or limited provider options.

**What you can do:**
- Confirm service area coverage before you finalize your referral. A reputable agency will tell you honestly whether they can serve a given address and how quickly.
- Ask about staffing depth, not just geographic reach. An agency that covers Germantown on paper but has limited caregiver availability there may not be able to meet a 48-hour discharge window.
- Build a referral network that includes multiple agencies with overlapping coverage areas, so you have a backup option when capacity is tight.

### 5. Language and Cultural Barriers

Maryland is one of the most linguistically diverse states in the country. Families in communities across Montgomery County may speak Spanish, Amharic, French, Korean, or dozens of other languages at home. When care coordinators and intake staff can only communicate in English, critical information gets lost — or families disengage entirely.

**What you can do:**
- Ask agencies directly about their multilingual capacity and whether they can match caregivers with families by language preference.
- Provide discharge instructions and home care orientation materials in the family's primary language when possible.
- Recognize that cultural fit — not just language — matters. A family's values around privacy, gender roles in caregiving, and dietary practices all affect whether a care relationship will actually work.

### 6. Trust Deficits and Prior Negative Experiences

Some families have had bad experiences with home care in the past — a caregiver who didn't show up, an agency that was hard to reach, a situation that felt unsafe. Those experiences make them reluctant to try again, even when they clearly need support.

**What you can do:**
- Acknowledge the concern directly. Don't dismiss prior experiences.
- Point families toward agencies with verifiable credentials — Maryland Department of Health OHCQ licensure, third-party quality ratings, and employer recognition programs that reflect caregiver satisfaction and retention.
- Encourage families to ask hard questions: What is your backup plan if our caregiver calls out? How do you supervise care? What happens if we're not happy?

## Building a Referral Partnership That Actually Works

The most effective discharge planners and social workers don't just hand off referrals — they build genuine working relationships with home care agencies. That means knowing who to call, what information they need, and how they communicate.

When evaluating a home care partner in Maryland, look for:

- **Maryland OHCQ licensure** — non-negotiable. Any agency serving patients in Maryland must be licensed by the Maryland Department of Health.
- **Clear intake process** — a responsive agency should be able to begin the intake conversation within hours of a referral, not days.
- **Transparent capacity** — good partners tell you when they're at capacity rather than accepting referrals they can't staff.
- **Family-centered communication** — caregivers who can communicate well with both the patient and the family, in a language they understand.
- **Quality indicators** — look for agencies that operate to recognized standards and can speak to caregiver training, supervision, and retention.

## A Note on Supporting Family Caregivers

One barrier that often goes unacknowledged in discharge planning is **caregiver burnout**. When a family member is already exhausted — from visiting the hospital daily, managing medications, fielding calls from providers — they may resist adding home care coordination to their list. Sometimes the barrier to access isn't the patient at all; it's a family system that is stretched to its limit.

Connecting families to respite care options, and helping them understand that professional in-home support is a relief rather than a burden, is some of the most important work a discharge planner or social worker can do.
