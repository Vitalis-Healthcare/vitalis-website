# Referring Patients to Home Care: Legal and Ethical Considerations for Maryland Professionals

For discharge planners, social workers, and care coordinators across Montgomery County and the broader Maryland region, **referring patients to home care** is one of the most consequential decisions you make. Done well, it protects patient safety, preserves dignity, and reduces costly readmissions. Done carelessly, it can expose your organization — and your professional license — to serious legal and ethical risk.

This guide walks through the key considerations you need to keep in mind every time you make a home care referral.

---

## Why Home Care Referrals Carry Real Legal Weight

A referral feels routine. But the moment you direct a patient or family toward a specific home care provider, you are exercising professional influence over a vulnerable person's care. That influence is governed by federal law, Maryland state law, and the ethical codes of your profession.

Whether you work at a hospital in Silver Spring, a rehabilitation facility in Rockville, or a social services office in Gaithersburg, the same framework applies.

---

## Federal Law: The Anti-Kickback Statute and Stark Law

Two federal laws shape almost every professional referral in healthcare.

### The Anti-Kickback Statute (AKS)

The **Anti-Kickback Statute** prohibits offering, paying, soliciting, or receiving anything of value — directly or indirectly — to induce referrals for services covered by Medicare, Medicaid, or other federal healthcare programs. The law is intentionally broad.

This means:
- Accepting gifts, meals, event tickets, or other items of value from a home care agency in exchange for referrals is prohibited
- Informal arrangements — even those that feel like simple professional courtesies — can cross the line
- Both the person giving and the person receiving can face criminal liability

The Office of Inspector General (OIG) has repeatedly emphasized that these rules apply to discharge planners and social workers, not just physicians.

### Stark Law

**Stark Law** (the Physician Self-Referral Law) primarily governs physicians, but its underlying principle — that financial relationships must not drive referral decisions — reflects a standard that applies broadly across the healthcare workforce.

If your facility or organization has any financial relationship with a home care agency, that relationship must be disclosed and evaluated for compliance.

---

## Maryland State Law and OHCQ Oversight

At the state level, **Maryland-licensed home care agencies** are regulated by the Maryland Department of Health's Office of Health Care Quality (OHCQ). When you refer to a licensed agency, you have a baseline assurance that the provider meets minimum state standards for staffing, training, and operations.

Referring to an unlicensed or out-of-state provider operating without proper Maryland credentials creates liability exposure for your patient and potentially for you. Always verify that an agency holds a current Maryland OHCQ license before making a referral.

Maryland's Health-General Article also includes provisions protecting patient rights in care transitions. Patients have the right to:
- Receive complete information about their care options
- Choose their own provider free from coercion
- Have their preferences documented and respected

## Ethical Obligations Under Professional Codes

Legal compliance is the floor, not the ceiling. Your professional ethics codes set a higher standard.

### NASW Code of Ethics for Social Workers

The **National Association of Social Workers (NASW) Code of Ethics** requires social workers to:
- Act in the best interest of clients, not in the interest of an employer or referral partner
- Avoid conflicts of interest, or disclose and manage them transparently
- Respect client self-determination — meaning the patient, not the care team, ultimately chooses their provider

Social workers in Maryland who violate these standards risk complaints to the Maryland Board of Social Work Examiners and potential license action.

### ACMA Standards for Case Managers and Discharge Planners

The **American Case Management Association (ACMA)** and the **Case Management Society of America (CMSA)** both emphasize that **discharge planning referrals** must be:
- Based on patient-centered criteria, not institutional convenience
- Supported by documentation of patient choice
- Free from any arrangement that prioritizes provider relationships over patient outcomes

---

## The CMS Discharge Planning Rule

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) updated its **Conditions of Participation for discharge planning** in 2019. For hospitals, this rule is binding. Key requirements include:

- Patients must receive a list of **all Medicare-certified or state-licensed home health agencies** serving their area — not just the ones your facility prefers
- The list must be unbiased and not indicate any preference
- Patients and families must have the opportunity to choose their own provider
- Any financial interest your facility has in a listed agency must be clearly disclosed

If your hospital or facility restricts referrals to a preferred provider list in a way that limits patient choice, it may be out of compliance with this rule.

## Practical Steps for Ethically Sound Referrals

Knowing the rules matters. But the day-to-day practice of **referring patients to home care** in Maryland also requires good habits and clear documentation.

### Assess Needs Before Matching Providers

Before identifying any agency, complete a thorough needs assessment:
- What level of care does this patient require — companion care, personal care, or skilled nursing?
- What are the patient's language, cultural, and scheduling preferences?
- What does the family's support system look like?
- Are there financial or insurance constraints, including Medicaid eligibility?

### Verify Agency Credentials

Before recommending any home care agency to a patient in Silver Spring, Germantown, Takoma Park, or anywhere else in Montgomery County, confirm:
- Current Maryland OHCQ licensure
- Adequate liability and workers' compensation insurance
- Background check policies for all caregivers
- Whether the agency operates to recognized quality standards

### Document Patient Choice

Always document that the patient or their authorized representative was given options and made their own choice. This protects the patient, your organization, and your license.

### Disclose Any Relationships

If your facility has any formal or informal relationship with an agency — a marketing agreement, a facility partnership, or even a staff member who moonlights for an agency — disclose it. Transparency is both legally required and ethically correct.

### Follow Up After Referral

Your ethical responsibility doesn't end when the patient leaves the facility. A brief follow-up — even a phone call — to confirm that services started as planned helps catch problems early and reinforces continuity of care.

These aren't just quality markers. They're indicators that a referral to this agency will hold up to scrutiny if your decision is ever questioned.

## A Note on Preferred Provider Arrangements

Some facilities negotiate formal preferred provider agreements with home care agencies. These arrangements are not automatically illegal, but they must be structured carefully.

If you work in a facility with a preferred provider list, make sure:
- Patients are still offered a full, unbiased list of options as required by CMS rules
- The arrangement has been reviewed for AKS compliance
- Financial terms of any agreement are properly disclosed
- The preferred provider genuinely earns its position through quality outcomes — not just marketing relationships
